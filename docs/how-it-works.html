Technical brief · Simple Runner

How the app turns satellite noise into a pace you can trust

A plain-language tour of the app's construction and the arithmetic behind every number on screen — written so the "why" behind a jumpy split or a slow-to-appear speed is never a mystery.

Platform Android & iOS, one codebase Phase 1 of 3 — live tracking & GPX logging Reading time ~9 min
01

Executive summary

Simple Runner is a Flutter app — one Dart codebase that compiles to a native Android and a native iOS app, rather than two separate projects kept in sync by hand. It reads your phone's GPS roughly once a second, turns that stream of coordinates into speed, distance, pace and kilometre splits, and writes the raw route to a GPX file you could open in any mapping tool.

The distinctive engineering problem here is not the arithmetic — distance and speed are two lines of trigonometry — it's that consumer GPS occasionally lies. A phone can report a location half a kilometre from where you actually are, for one sample, before correcting itself. Unfiltered, that single bad sample would spike your speed to over 1,000 km/h and permanently inflate your total distance. Most of what follows is the app deciding, sample by sample, whether to believe what the GPS just told it.

02

Technology & construction

Four building blocks, each doing one job. None of them talk to the phone's GPS hardware directly except one — which is deliberate.

Framework
Flutter / Dart
Google's UI toolkit. One source tree compiles to native ARM binaries for both Android and iOS — no shared web view, no separate Swift/Kotlin apps to maintain in parallel.
GPS access
geolocator
The one package allowed to talk to the OS location APIs directly. Tuned per-platform: bestForNavigation accuracy, background updates enabled on iOS via an Info.plist entry.
State management
Riverpod
A one-way data pipeline: the GPS stream feeds a controller, the controller emits an immutable state object, and every widget on screen just redraws from that. No component reaches backward to mutate another's data.
Track storage
GPX 1.1 files
The open standard used by Garmin, Strava, and most mapping software. Every run is written to disk as a standard .gpx track — portable, and readable outside this app.

The data path, GPS fix to screen

Hardware
Phone GPS radio
Combines satellite, Wi-Fi and cell-tower signals into one position estimate, roughly once per second.
core/location
GeolocatorLocationService
Wraps the OS location API. Nothing outside this file ever sees a raw platform type — the rest of the app only knows a simple lat/lon/speed/accuracy shape.
domain/tracking
MetricsEngine
Pure calculation, no UI awareness. Accepts or rejects each fix, accumulates distance and time, detects kilometre crossings.
features/live_run
LiveRunController
Riverpod controller. Also drives the GPX writer and the screen-stays-on lock in parallel with the metrics engine.
UI
Metric tiles
Re-render automatically whenever the controller's state changes. No polling, no manual refresh calls.

Why the layers are separated

core/location The only place that imports the GPS plugin. Swapping GPS providers, or feeding it recorded test routes, touches one file.
domain/* Plain Dart — no Flutter, no UI import allowed. This is what makes the maths in §3 unit-testable in isolation, run in milliseconds, with no phone or simulator needed.
features/live_run The screen and its controller. Reads from domain, never the reverse — the calculation layer has no idea a screen exists.

This isn't architectural tidiness for its own sake — it's what let the outlier-rejection logic in §3 be tested against thousands of synthetic GPS traces with zero live GPS involved, and what let this session catch and fix three separate bugs before they were ever tested against your own runs.

The phone hands the app a raw stream of (latitude, longitude, timestamp, accuracy) readings. Everything you see on the tracking screen is derived from that stream — nothing is estimated from your step count or phone motion sensors.

One second is a request, not a guarantee — the OS and the GPS chipset decide the real cadence, and it varies with sky visibility. Open sky and a good lock: close to 1 fix/second. Under tree cover, between tall buildings, or on a cold start: gaps of several seconds are normal, and the app is built to tolerate them without the display freezing or jumping.

The core geometry

Every distance calculation starts with the haversine formula — the standard way to find the straight-line distance between two latitude/longitude points on a sphere. Given any two consecutive fixes, it returns the distance between them in metres; dividing that by the time between their timestamps gives the implied speed for that segment.

On that interpolation: say you're at 970 m at one fix and 1,050 m at the next, one second later. The 1 km mark falls 75% of the way through that segment, so the app estimates you crossed it 750 ms into that one-second gap — rather than crediting the whole split to whichever fix happened to land on either side of the line. This is also why a split time can differ slightly from what a simpler tracker would show for the identical GPS data.

04

Precautions against bad GPS data

GPS receivers occasionally hand back a wrong answer — most often right at the start of a run, before the chipset has a confident lock. Every fix passes through three checks before it's allowed to count.

GuardWhat it catchesThreshold
Accuracy filter The OS reports a confidence radius with every fix. A fix the phone itself isn't confident about is dropped before it reaches any calculation. reject if accuracy > 25 m
Plausible-speed filter No runner sustains this pace. A fix implying it is treated as a glitch — a stale or default location before the GPS gets a real lock — not real motion. reject if implied speed > 12 m/s (43 km/h)
Absolute-jump cap Closes a loophole in the speed filter alone: given a long enough gap since the last fix, any distance starts to look like a slow, plausible pace. This catches a large teleport regardless of how much time has passed. reject if jump > 20 km

What happens to a rejected fix. It's discarded from the live metrics — distance and average speed act as though it never arrived. But it isn't simply forgotten: if the next fix agrees with the rejected one (i.e. two consecutive readings that are mutually consistent, showing at least a couple of metres of real movement between them), the app concludes the earlier "good" fix was actually the mistake, and quietly re-anchors its tracking onto the new, more plausible position — crediting no extra distance or time for the jump itself, the same way a pause and resume works.

That recovery step exists because the obvious alternative — always trusting the last accepted fix — has a sharp failure mode: if that fix turns out to have been the bad one, every subsequent real fix near your actual position also looks like an impossible jump and gets rejected too, until enough time passes for the maths to catch up. An early version of this logic quarantined 77 seconds of perfectly good running data this way before the fix below it.

The GPX file written to disk, by contrast, keeps every fix, filtered or not — filtering is a display decision the app makes for you live; the underlying recording stays complete, in case you ever want to re-process it differently.

05

What to expect in the real world

GPS accuracy on a phone is a function of sky visibility, not the app. These are realistic bands for a modern smartphone, not lab conditions.

Open sky · clear conditions
Accuracy typically ±3–8 m. Speed and pace settle within a few seconds of starting and track smoothly. This is the everyday case for most road and park running.
Urban canyon · tall buildings
Signal reflects off buildings before reaching the phone ("multipath"), inflating accuracy to ±15–30 m. Pace readings get noisier; more fixes sit near the 25 m accuracy cutoff and get dropped.
Tree cover · narrow trails
Fix interval stretches — expect gaps of 3–8 s between updates rather than a steady 1 s. Distance still accumulates correctly; the current-speed reading updates less often.
First 10–20 seconds of any run
The GPS chipset is still narrowing its lock. This is precisely the window the outlier filters in §4 exist for — an early bad fix here is the most common real-world trigger.

A rule of thumb: if a single split or momentary speed reading looks implausible, distance and average speed are almost always still correct — the filters described in §4 exist specifically to protect those cumulative numbers, even when one input sample was briefly wrong. It's the instantaneous readings — current speed, in-progress split pace — that are most exposed to real-time GPS noise, by nature of updating every second rather than averaging over the whole run.
